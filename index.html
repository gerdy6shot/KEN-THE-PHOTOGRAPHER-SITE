The Legacy Narrative

A Half-Century Through the Lens

For more than 55 years, Kenneth “Kenny” Harris—professionally known as KENTHEPHOTOGRAPHER (KTP)—has used photography as a record of memory, culture, movement, and survival.

His archive stretches across Black music, sports, politics, community life, celebrity, and protest. From Carnegie Hall to Madison Square Garden, from neighborhood streets in South Jamaica, Queens to the Million Man March, Harris has photographed both the people who shaped history and the people who lived through it.

Born in 1955 at Queens General Hospital and raised in the 40 Projects of South Jamaica, his relationship with photography began early. At five years old, he helped his father, Jack Harris, in the darkroom, spreading chemical fixer. After his father’s death, his eldest brother, Ptah Hotep, placed a Canon rangefinder in his hands.

By 15, Harris was photographing artists including Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin, and Nina Simone. At 16, he found his way onto the floor and bench area at Madison Square Garden to photograph professional basketball. By 17, he was documenting the 1973 funeral of Clifford Glover, placing his camera inside one of the defining civil-rights stories of New York City.

A Life Beyond the Camera

Photography is only one part of Kenny Harris’s story. He describes himself through several lives lived at once: photographer, athlete, community organizer, distance runner, and public servant.

As KTP, he documented cultural icons, political movements, sporting events, and everyday life. As a basketball player and youth organizer, he helped create community through tournaments at IS 8 and Montebello Park. As a distance runner, he became known for an unconventional training style—logging miles through New York while carrying 10-pound hand weights overhead. He also spent years working for the New York City Parks Department as a pool filter plant operator.

These identities were never separate from the photographer. They shaped the way he moved through the city, the people he could reach, and the moments he recognized as worth preserving.

The Second Chapter

In 1974, Harris stepped away from photography. The pause lasted roughly two decades.

Then, in 1995, his brother Ptah urged him to photograph the Million Man March in Washington, D.C. Harris returned to the camera with full force. He remained on the National Mall for approximately 20 hours, photographing continuously and exposing 20 rolls of film.

That day became the beginning of the second chapter of his photographic life.

Over the decades that followed, Harris expanded his archive alongside his wife and creative partner, Cynthia Horner, longtime publisher and CEO associated with Right On! and Word Up! magazines. Together, they co-founded Cinnamon Flix and Right On Media LLC and continued documenting music, entertainment, politics, and Black cultural life.

His archive includes figures and moments connected to James Brown, Aaliyah, Jay-Z, Venus and Serena Williams, Michael Jackson’s memorial, Barack and Michelle Obama, and generations of artists, athletes, activists, and public figures.

His photography has appeared across outlets including The Amsterdam News, CNN, Hip Hop Weekly, and the Showtime series The Supreme Team.

The Archive Is the Legacy

Today, the most important work may be preservation. Thousands of negatives, contact sheets, prints, and uncut rolls of film remain within the Kenneth Harris archive—many of them documenting moments that have never been publicly seen.

The goal is no longer simply to show photographs. It is to preserve a visual history.

Through ongoing archival work and collaboration with initiatives including the Queens Public Library Memory Project, Harris is working to ensure that the people, places, movements, and memories contained in his photographs remain accessible beyond his lifetime.

His archive is not only the story of one photographer. It is a record of a city, a culture, and more than half a century of American life.
